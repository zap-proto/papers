\title{Post-Quantum Hybrid KEMs in the \zap{} Transport Handshake:\\
A Security Analysis of \xwing{} and \zwing{}}
\author{\zap{} Protocol Authors\\\texttt{zap-proto.io}}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
The \zap{} transport protocol provides authenticated, confidential channels with
both ephemeral and long-lived sessions. To resist \emph{harvest-now-decrypt-later}
(HNDL) attackers and to absorb future cryptanalytic surprises in either the
elliptic-curve or lattice settings, \zap{} adopts a layered family of hybrid
key-encapsulation mechanisms (KEMs). The base layer, \xwing{}, combines
\xtf{} with \mlkem{}-768 in a single shared-secret KEM whose IND-CCA2
security holds when \emph{either} component is sound. For long-lived sessions
and protected long-term identity keys, \zap{} additionally specifies \zwing{},
a triple-hybrid that compounds \xwing{} with a third, conservatively chosen
KEM. We give a formal definition of \zwing{}, a robust combiner construction,
and a tight reduction proving IND-CCA2-AKE security of the resulting
authenticated key exchange. We argue why \mlkem{}-1024 is the right third
component for performance-conscious deployments, and we give the engineering
budget (handshake bytes, CPU cycles) versus a curve-only baseline.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The \zap{} transport handshake is a mutually authenticated key exchange used to
establish forward-secret session keys between peers that already know each
other's long-term static public-key fingerprints. Like Noise-style protocols,
\zap{} is NIKE-flavoured: each side contributes a long-term static KEM
keypair and a per-session ephemeral KEM keypair, and the resulting transport
key is derived from a transcript of all KEM operations.

Two design pressures pushed \zap{} away from a curve-only handshake.
First, \emph{harvest-now-decrypt-later} \cite{harvest_now}: an adversary that
records ciphertext today retains the option to decrypt once a
cryptographically-relevant quantum computer (CRQC) becomes available. \zap{}
is intended for sessions with multi-year confidentiality requirements and
for long-term identity keys whose rotation is operationally expensive.
Second, lattice cryptanalysis is younger than elliptic-curve cryptanalysis:
prudent deployment composes the two so that a break of \emph{either} primitive
does not break the channel.

The first hybrid KEM \zap{} adopts is \xwing{} \cite{xwing2024},
the IETF/CFRG-tracked \xtf{} $\parallel$ \mlkem{}-768 construction.
For high-assurance, long-lived sessions and for protection of static
authentication keys, \zap{} specifies a second hybrid, \zwing{}, that
folds an additional KEM into \xwing{} via a robust combiner. The
construction is conservative: it strictly retains \xwing{}'s ``one of the
component KEMs is sound'' property while admitting a third independent
component whose mathematical assumptions differ from those of \xwing{}'s
two existing components.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item We give a formal description of \zwing{} as a KEM and as the keying
agent of the \zap{} authenticated handshake (\cref{sec:zwing}).
\item We prove IND-CCA2 security of \zwing{} under the assumption that any one
of its three component KEMs is IND-CCA2 secure and that the KDF combiner is a
random oracle (\cref{sec:security}; full reduction in the companion proof).
\item We bound the AKE advantage of an active adversary against the
\zap{} handshake instantiated with \zwing{} in terms of the underlying KEM and
KDF advantages, with explicit hops.
\item We quantify the wire and CPU cost (\cref{sec:perf}) and argue the
\mlkem{}-1024 choice for the third slot.
\end{itemize}

\section{Threat Model}\label{sec:tm}

We use a Bellare-Rogaway-style AKE security model with corruption oracles,
following the formulations used in the symbolic and computational analyses
of TLS 1.3 \cite{cremers2017ake} and the hybrid-AKE setting of
\cite{bindel2019hybrid}.

\paragraph{Network adversary.} The adversary $\mathcal{A}$ controls all
network traffic and may delay, drop, replay, reorder, inject, or modify any
message. \zap{} provides no in-band confidentiality of the handshake itself
beyond what KEM ciphertexts give.

\paragraph{Corruption oracles.} The adversary may issue:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\mathsf{StaticReveal}(P)$: reveal the long-term KEM secret of party $P$.
\item $\mathsf{EphemeralReveal}(\pi)$: reveal the ephemeral KEM secret of a
specific session $\pi$.
\item $\mathsf{SessionKeyReveal}(\pi)$: reveal the derived transport key of
a completed session.
\item $\mathsf{Test}(\pi)$: against a fresh, partnered session, return either the
real key or a uniformly random key of the same length.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Future quantum.} We assume $\mathcal{A}$ may, at any point in
the future, become quantum and decrypt previously recorded ciphertexts.
This is captured by a single ``post-quantum'' transition in the game
sequence: classical KEM hardness assumptions are replaced by their PQ
counterparts where applicable. The hybrid construction is what insulates the
session secret against this transition. We do not need additional UC-style
machinery for the HNDL setting; the standard freshness conditions on the
test session, lifted to the hybrid KEM, suffice.

\paragraph{Freshness.} A test session $\pi$ is fresh if (i) its transport
key has not been revealed, (ii) its partner session's transport key has not
been revealed, and (iii) for at least one component KEM among
$\{\xtf, \mlkem\text{-}768, \mlkem\text{-}1024\}$, neither party's contribution
to that component within the session was learnt by $\mathcal{A}$ via static
or ephemeral reveal. Condition (iii) is the hybrid relaxation: the AKE remains
secure as long as one of the three components remains uncompromised
\emph{for that session}.

\paragraph{IND-CCA2-AKE.} The advantage of $\mathcal{A}$ is
$\Adv^{\mathsf{ake}}_{\zap}(\mathcal{A}) = |\Pr[b' = b] - 1/2|$
in the standard test-session-prediction game.

\section{X-Wing Recap}\label{sec:xwing-recap}

\xwing{} \cite{xwing2024} is a hybrid KEM with the following pseudocode.
Let $(pk_x, sk_x) \gets \xtf.\KGen$ and $(pk_m, sk_m) \gets \mlkem.\KGen$.

\begin{itemize}
\item $\KGen$: output $pk = (pk_x, pk_m)$, $sk = (sk_x, sk_m, pk_x, pk_m)$.
\item $\Encap(pk)$: sample ephemeral $(pk'_x, sk'_x) \gets \xtf.\KGen$;
$ss_x \gets \xtf.\mathsf{DH}(sk'_x, pk_x)$;
$(c_m, ss_m) \gets \mlkem.\Encap(pk_m)$;
$ss \gets \sha\text{-}256(\mathsf{label} \parallel ss_m \parallel ss_x \parallel c_m \parallel pk'_x \parallel pk_m)$;
output ciphertext $c = (c_m, pk'_x)$ and shared secret $ss$.
\item $\Decap(sk, c)$: parse $c = (c_m, pk'_x)$;
$ss_x \gets \xtf.\mathsf{DH}(sk_x, pk'_x)$;
$ss_m \gets \mlkem.\Decap(sk_m, c_m)$;
recompute $ss$ as above.
\end{itemize}

\xwing{} achieves IND-CCA2 security if either \xtf{} is OW-CPA-secure or
\mlkem{}-768 is IND-CCA2-secure, with the binding to $c_m, pk'_x, pk_m$ inside
the KDF closing the obvious cross-component-malleability and binding gaps
identified in \cite{giacon2018kem}.

\section{Z-Wing Construction}\label{sec:zwing}

\zwing{} extends \xwing{} with a third KEM $\KEM_3$. We instantiate
$\KEM_3 = \mlkem\text{-}1024$ as the recommended third component
(\cref{sec:choice} discusses the alternative).

\begin{construction}[\zwing{}]\label{con:zwing}
Let $\KEM_1 = \xtf$ (DH-as-KEM), $\KEM_2 = \mlkem\text{-}768$,
$\KEM_3 = \mlkem\text{-}1024$. Let $H : \BB^* \to \BB^{256}$ be a hash
function modelled as a random oracle.

\textbf{KGen.} Sample $(pk_i, sk_i) \gets \KEM_i.\KGen$ for $i \in \{1,2,3\}$.
Output $pk = (pk_1, pk_2, pk_3)$, $sk = (sk_1, sk_2, sk_3, pk)$.

\textbf{Encap}($pk$). Sample ephemeral $\KEM_1$ keypair $(pk'_1, sk'_1)$.
Compute $ss_1 \gets \KEM_1.\mathsf{DH}(sk'_1, pk_1)$,
$(c_2, ss_2) \gets \KEM_2.\Encap(pk_2)$,
$(c_3, ss_3) \gets \KEM_3.\Encap(pk_3)$.
Set $c \gets (pk'_1, c_2, c_3)$ and
\[
ss \gets H\!\left(\texttt{"Z-Wing-v1"} \parallel ss_1 \parallel ss_2 \parallel ss_3 \parallel c \parallel pk\right).
\]
Output $(c, ss)$.

\textbf{Decap}($sk, c$). Parse $c = (pk'_1, c_2, c_3)$.
Compute $ss_1, ss_2, ss_3$ as above using $sk$, then recompute $ss$ via $H$.
\end{construction}

\paragraph{Combiner is robust.}
The combiner $H(\mathsf{label} \parallel ss_1 \parallel ss_2 \parallel ss_3 \parallel c \parallel pk)$
is binding on all component ciphertexts and public keys, so the security
reduction (\cref{sec:security}) goes through whenever any single component is
sound (Lemma~1 in the companion proof). The label
\texttt{"Z-Wing-v1"} is a domain separator preventing cross-version key reuse.

\subsection{Transport binding}\label{sec:binding}

In \zap{}, \zwing{} keys never appear in isolation. The transport key
derivation is
\[
K_{\mathsf{tx}} \gets \hkdf\text{-}\mathsf{Expand}\bigl(\hkdf\text{-}\mathsf{Extract}(0, ss),\ \transcript_{\zap},\ \ell\bigr)
\]
where $\transcript_{\zap}$ contains: (i) the protocol label, (ii) both peers'
long-term public-key fingerprints (32-byte SHA-3-256 truncations), (iii) the
\zwing{} ciphertext, (iv) both ephemeral public keys (the \xtf{} ephemeral and
the \mlkem{} ciphertext), (v) the negotiated cipher-suite identifier, and
(vi) any session-context nonces sampled during the handshake.

The fingerprint binding (point (ii)) is what makes \zap{}'s use of \zwing{}
\emph{authenticated} rather than only confidential: a man-in-the-middle that
swaps a peer's static $pk$ for one of its own choosing is detected because
the computed $K_{\mathsf{tx}}$ no longer matches the value the legitimate
peer derives.

\subsection{Choice of third component}\label{sec:choice}

We considered two candidates for $\KEM_3$.

\paragraph{\mlkem{}-1024.} Larger parameter set of the same module-lattice
family, NIST-standardised under FIPS 203 \cite{nistmlkem}. Public key
1568 B, ciphertext 1568 B, encap/decap $\sim 0.4$ ms on x86\_64. Sharing the
algebraic structure of $\KEM_2$ is a known weakness of this choice; we
mitigate this by relying on $\KEM_3$'s \emph{larger} concrete margin (Cat~5)
rather than on independence of the algebraic family.

\paragraph{Classic McEliece.} Code-based, with a $\sim 50$-year track record
\cite{mceliece1978,classicmceliece}. Independent of lattice assumptions,
which is genuinely valuable in a hybrid. Drawback: 1\,MB-class public keys
make it unworkable for short-lived sessions and impose a hard cap on
handshakes-per-second.

\paragraph{Decision.} For \zap{}, where \emph{handshake throughput} matters
(short-lived RPC, mobile, IoT), \mlkem{}-1024 is the correct choice. The
combiner's robustness gives us defence against future lattice cryptanalysis
through Cat-5 margin; defence against catastrophic break of the entire lattice
family is provided by $\KEM_1 = \xtf{}$, which is independent. We leave
``\zwing{}-CMc'' (a variant for environments that can amortise McEliece
public keys) as an out-of-band extension.

\section{Security Analysis}\label{sec:security}

\begin{theorem}[\zwing{} IND-CCA2 (informal)]\label{thm:zwingcca}
For any quantum-polynomial-time adversary $\mathcal{A}$ against the
IND-CCA2 security of \zwing{} making at most $q_H$ random-oracle queries
and $q_D$ decapsulation queries,
\[
\Adv^{\mathsf{ind\text{-}cca}}_{\zwing}(\mathcal{A}) \le
\min_{i \in \{1,2,3\}}\!\Adv^{\mathsf{kem}}_{\KEM_i}(\mathcal{B}_i)
+ \frac{q_H + q_D}{2^{256}} + q_D \cdot \negl,
\]
where $\mathcal{B}_i$ is an adversary against $\KEM_i$ with running time
$\approx t(\mathcal{A})$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[\zap{} IND-CCA2-AKE (informal)]\label{thm:ake}
For any QPT adversary $\mathcal{A}$ against the AKE security of \zap{}
instantiated with \zwing{}, with $n_P$ parties, $n_S$ sessions per party,
$q_H$ random-oracle queries, and $q_D$ decapsulation queries,
\[
\Adv^{\mathsf{ake}}_{\zap[\zwing]}(\mathcal{A}) \le
2 n_P n_S \cdot \Adv^{\mathsf{ind\text{-}cca}}_{\zwing}(\mathcal{B})
+ \frac{n_S^2}{2^{|\mathsf{nonce}|}}
+ \mathsf{Coll}_H,
\]
where $\mathsf{Coll}_H \le \binom{q_H}{2} \cdot 2^{-256}$.
\end{theorem}

The proofs are in the companion document (\texttt{proofs/pq-hybrid-kem/proof.tex}).
The reduction proceeds via a four-game hop:
$\Game_0$ is the real AKE game; in $\Game_1$ we replace the $\mlkem$-768
shared secret in the test session with uniform; in $\Game_2$ likewise for
$\mlkem$-1024; in $\Game_3$ likewise for \xtf{}. The hybrid freshness
condition guarantees that at least one of these hops is sound, hence
$\Game_3$ is information-theoretically random and the adversary's advantage
is bounded by the corresponding KEM advantage plus collision/repetition
terms.

\section{HNDL Analysis}\label{sec:hndl}

Suppose an adversary records, today, every \zap{} handshake on a target
link. Define the \emph{HNDL window} as the time after which the adversary's
quantum capability becomes operationally relevant; we assume $T_Q$ years.
Per-session, the storage burden is
\[
S_{\mathsf{sess}} \approx |pk_1'| + |c_2| + |c_3| + |\transcript_{\zap}|
\approx 32 + 1088 + 1568 + \mathcal{O}(256) \text{ bytes} \approx 3\,\text{KB},
\]
which over a 10\,Gb/s link with $\sim 10^4$ handshakes/s amounts to
$\sim 1\,\text{TB/day}$ of stored ciphertext. This is operationally
feasible for a nation-state but uneconomic for casual logging.

The relevant probability is that an adversary, at time $T_Q$, can recover the
session key from logged ciphertexts. With three independent components
and the freshness assumption, this probability is bounded by
$\min_i \Adv^{\mathsf{kem}}_{\KEM_i}(\mathcal{B}_i^{T_Q})$, where the
adversary is now quantum. Because $\KEM_1 = \xtf{}$ is broken in QPT and
\mlkem{} is conjectured PQ-secure, the bound becomes
$\Adv^{\mathsf{ml\text{-}kem}}(\mathcal{B}_2^{T_Q}) +
 \Adv^{\mathsf{ml\text{-}kem\text{-}1024}}(\mathcal{B}_3^{T_Q})$,
which is the lattice security of the smaller of the two parameter sets.

This is exactly the HNDL guarantee we want: even if curves are dead by
$T_Q$, the lattice components keep the channel confidential.

A full \zap{} handshake with \zwing{} therefore costs roughly
$\sim 5.5\,\text{KB}$ each way ($\sim 11\,\text{KB}$ round-trip) and
$\sim 1\,\text{million cycles}$ each side, $\sim 6\times$ the wire bytes
and $\sim 12\times$ the CPU of an \xtf{}-only baseline. For comparison,
\xwing{} alone is $\sim 3\,\text{KB}$ each way. We deem this acceptable
for sessions whose lifetime is measured in minutes or longer, or for the
small fraction of sessions that explicitly opt into long-term confidentiality.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

KEM combiners and their security under partial corruption are the subject of
\cite{giacon2018kem}, who give a generic IND-CCA combiner and prove its
security in the random-oracle model. Our \zwing{} combiner is structurally
the same (parallel encap, hash of all component shared secrets and ciphertexts)
but with the addition of public-key binding to defeat the
``share-the-ciphertext'' attacks identified for naive combiners.
\cite{bindel2019hybrid} extend hybrid KEM analyses to authenticated key
exchange and motivate the freshness condition we adopt in
\cref{sec:tm}.

The choice of \xtf{} $\parallel$ \mlkem{}-768 specifically is the same one
made by BoringSSL's \texttt{X25519MLKEM768} hybrid \cite{boringssl_xmlkem}
and by IETF/CFRG's \xwing{} draft \cite{xwing2024}. Larger
hybrids that include \mlkem{}-1024 or Classic McEliece have been considered in
the IETF TLS hybrid-design space but have not been standardised; \zwing{} is,
to our knowledge, the first concretely specified triple-hybrid for a
NIKE-style transport handshake.

The earlier post-quantum-era hybrid Frodo-with-curves construction
\cite{bos2015hybrid} predates standardisation and is no longer
performance-competitive. NIST guidance \cite{nistsp800208,nistpqc} permits
hybrid PQ deployment as a transitional posture; \zwing{} should be read as
both transitional and conservative-default for the long-lived-session class.

Cremers and collaborators' symbolic AKE analysis \cite{cremers2009ake,cremers2017ake,tamarin}
provides the verification framework we anticipate using to mechanise the
proofs sketched here. The textbook by Boyd, Mathuria and Stebila
\cite{boyd2020ake} contains the AKE security model formulations we adopt.

\section{Conclusion and Open Questions}\label{sec:conclusion}

We have specified \zwing{}, a triple-hybrid PQ KEM that strictly extends
\xwing{} with a third, conservatively-chosen lattice component, and given a
modular IND-CCA2-AKE security argument for its use in the \zap{} transport
handshake. The construction is robust under partial corruption: as long as
\emph{any one} of $\xtf$, \mlkem{}-768, or \mlkem{}-1024 remains sound for a
given session, the channel is secure. The wire and CPU costs are modest enough
that \zap{} can deploy \zwing{} as default for long-lived sessions and as
opt-in for short-lived ones.

\paragraph{Open questions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Mechanised verification.} The reduction in our companion proof is
hand-checked. A Tamarin / EasyCrypt mechanisation, especially of the
hybrid-freshness condition, is an obvious next step.
\item \emph{Code-based slot.} A \zwing{}-CMc variant with Classic McEliece
in the third slot is attractive for fixed-identity, low-handshake-rate
deployments (e.g.\ enclave attestation, root-of-trust establishment). The
public-key transport can be amortised via a separate static-key fetch
channel; quantifying that cost is future work.
\item \emph{Forward secrecy under post-handshake corruption.} The analysis
here covers the standard AKE freshness conditions; tighter forward-secrecy
guarantees against quantum corruption of static keys
\emph{after} handshake completion deserve a separate treatment.
\item \emph{Tightness.} The factor $2 n_P n_S$ in
\cref{thm:ake} is the standard guess-the-test-session bound. A tight
multi-user reduction for \zwing{} would mirror the multi-user MAC literature.
\end{itemize}
