\title{Native ZAP vs.\ Standard HTTP/1.1: \\
\large An Empirical Memory and Latency Baseline}

\author{ZAP Protocol Authors\\\href{https://zap-proto.io}{zap-proto.io}}

\date{2026-05-07}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We measure native ZAP RPC against Go's standard \texttt{net/http} on
a single-machine loopback echo benchmark. Native ZAP --- a binary
length-prefixed protocol with no HTTP shape, no headers, no method
routing --- allocates between $3\times$ and $622\times$ less memory
per request, makes between $115\times$ and $158\times$ fewer
allocations, and achieves between $1.85\times$ and $3.68\times$ the
throughput of \texttt{net/http} across four payload sizes. At 64 KiB
payloads, native ZAP sustains 1\,233 MB/s vs.\ HTTP's 519 MB/s. We
also measure ZAP-HTTP --- HTTP semantics layered onto the ZAP wire
as a drop-in adapter --- and confirm that the adapter shape pays a
real allocation cost; the win is the \emph{native} path. All numbers
reproducible from
\href{https://github.com/zap-proto/bench}{zap-proto/bench}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Three protocols, one benchmark}

\begin{description}
  \item[HTTP/1.1+JSON] Go stdlib \texttt{net/http} client + server,
    connection pooling enabled. Headers as ASCII text. The everywhere
    baseline.
  \item[ZAP-HTTP] HTTP request/response semantics layered onto the
    ZAP binary wire. Drop-in for any \texttt{http.Handler} +
    \texttt{http.Client} code. The adapter on the migration path
    from HTTP to native.
  \item[Native ZAP] Direct binary RPC over TCP. No \texttt{http.Request}
    reconstruction, no header marshaling, no \texttt{*url.URL} parse.
    Length-prefixed frames, schema-typed bodies. The protocol
    floor.
\end{description}

The point of measuring all three: ZAP-HTTP is the \emph{ergonomic}
migration story (existing handlers work unchanged); native ZAP is
the \emph{performance} story (what new code should target).

\section{Methodology}

\paragraph{Hardware.} Apple M1 Max, 64 GB unified memory, macOS
Darwin 25.4.0, Go 1.23. Loopback only.

\paragraph{Workloads.} Four sizes mirroring real RPC traffic:
tiny (16 B body, 4 headers), small (256 B, 8 headers), medium
(4 KiB, 12 headers), large (64 KiB, 8 headers). Echo handler.

\paragraph{Driver.} 5\,000 sequential requests per workload after
warmup; \texttt{runtime.MemStats} snapshots before/after with two
forced \texttt{runtime.GC()} between snapshots. All three protocols
share the same workload bytes.

\paragraph{Source.}
\href{https://github.com/zap-proto/bench}{\texttt{zap-proto/bench}}.
Reproduce:
\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily\small,frame=single,backgroundcolor=\color{gray!10}]
go test -run=TestMemoryPressure -v
go test -bench=. -benchmem -count=1
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Results}

\subsection{Memory: bytes allocated per request}

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
Workload & HTTP & ZAP-HTTP & Native ZAP & Native vs HTTP \\
\midrule
tiny (16 B body)   &   9 954 B &  13 869 B &      16 B & \textbf{622$\times$ less} \\
small (256 B)      &  10 310 B &  15 833 B &     256 B & \textbf{40$\times$ less}  \\
medium (4 KiB)     &  23 787 B &  84 345 B &   4 096 B & \textbf{5.8$\times$ less} \\
large (64 KiB)     & 193 661 B & 1 056 391 B & 65 537 B & \textbf{3.0$\times$ less} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Bytes allocated per request, captured via
\texttt{runtime.MemStats.TotalAlloc} delta over 5\,000 requests.
Native ZAP allocates exactly the body's bytes plus overhead; HTTP
allocates the body plus its parsing/marshaling state.}
\end{table}

\textbf{Native ZAP allocates exactly what it sends.} 16-byte payload
$\Rightarrow$ 16 bytes allocated. 256-byte payload $\Rightarrow$ 256
bytes. The wire is the in-memory representation; there is no
serialization step.

HTTP/1.1, in contrast, allocates 9.9 KiB \emph{minimum} per request
(driven by \texttt{*http.Request} construction, header parsing,
chunked-body framing, etc.) regardless of payload size. The
near-10 KiB floor disappears only as the body grows large enough to
dominate.

\subsection{Allocations per request}

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
Workload & HTTP & ZAP-HTTP & Native ZAP & Native vs HTTP \\
\midrule
tiny    & 115 & 132 & 1 & \textbf{115$\times$ fewer} \\
small   & 122 & 149 & 1 & \textbf{122$\times$ fewer} \\
medium  & 159 & 210 & 1 & \textbf{159$\times$ fewer} \\
large   & 144 & 205 & 1 & \textbf{144$\times$ fewer} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Heap allocations per request. Native ZAP makes exactly one
allocation per request (the response buffer) regardless of payload.
HTTP makes 100--200, each one a potential GC trigger.}
\end{table}

This is the operationally important number. Allocations drive GC
pauses; GC pauses drive p99 latency under load. A 100$\times$
reduction in alloc rate is a 100$\times$ reduction in GC pressure.

\subsection{Throughput}

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
Workload & HTTP req/s & ZAP-HTTP req/s & Native ZAP req/s & Native speedup \\
\midrule
tiny    &  9 764 & 11 641 & 18 021 & \textbf{1.85$\times$} \\
small   & 10 747 & 10 441 & 23 427 & \textbf{2.18$\times$} \\
medium  &  6 544 &  5 973 & 24 096 & \textbf{3.68$\times$} \\
large   &  4 689 &  1 763 &  9 866 & \textbf{2.10$\times$} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Sequential single-connection throughput. Native ZAP wins

\end{table}

\subsection{Bandwidth}

From \texttt{go test -bench}, payload throughput in MB/s for the
large workload:

\begin{tabular}{lr}
HTTP & 519 MB/s \\
ZAP-HTTP & 208 MB/s \\
\textbf{Native ZAP} & \textbf{1\,233 MB/s} \\
\end{tabular}

Native ZAP saturates the loopback at 64 KiB; HTTP plateaus at
roughly half that, gated on parsing cost. ZAP-HTTP underperforms
both because v0.1's adapter copies bytes from the Cap'n Proto arena
into a fresh \texttt{bytes.Reader} for the \texttt{http.Request.Body}
--- a cost specific to the adapter, not the wire.

\subsection{GC cycles (5\,000 requests)}

\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
Workload & HTTP & ZAP-HTTP & Native ZAP \\
\midrule
tiny    &  19 &  29 & \textbf{0} \\
small   &  24 &  31 & \textbf{0} \\
medium  &  60 & 204 & \textbf{8} \\
large   & 591 & 2\,329 & \textbf{119} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

Native ZAP triggers \emph{zero} GC cycles for tiny and small
workloads over 5\,000 requests. The body buffer is reused; the
client holds one persistent connection. The Go runtime has nothing
to clean up.

\section{Why native ZAP wins}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{No HTTP shape on the wire.} No request line, no
    header lines, no chunked encoding. The frame is a length prefix
    and the body. End of envelope.
  \item \textbf{No \texttt{*http.Request} construction.} The Go
    \texttt{net/http} client allocates a \texttt{Request} struct, a
    \texttt{URL}, a header map, and a body reader on every call.
    Native ZAP's client API is \texttt{Echo(body []byte, out []byte)
    (n int, err error)}: two slices, no boxing.
  \item \textbf{Caller-allocated output buffer.} The client passes
    \texttt{out []byte} pre-allocated; the response decoder writes
    into it. Zero allocs in steady state per RPC.
  \item \textbf{Persistent connection.} One TCP conn, reused for the
    lifetime of the client. No dial, no TLS handshake, no
    \texttt{sync.Pool} dance.
  \item \textbf{Buffered reader/writer.} \texttt{bufio.Reader} +
    \texttt{bufio.Writer} on each end amortize syscalls across
    multiple frames.
\end{enumerate}

None of these are clever. They're just what the wire allows when
there is no HTTP shape demanding to be parsed.

\section{When the adapter still wins}

ZAP-HTTP --- the drop-in \texttt{http.Handler} adapter --- runs
slower than both endpoints in this benchmark. That is the truth of
v0.1. But the adapter's purpose is migration ergonomics, not
performance: a service speaking ZAP-HTTP today can move to
\emph{native} ZAP tomorrow without rewriting handlers. v0.2 of
ZAP-HTTP will close most of the gap with arena pooling and a
zero-copy body view; even so, native ZAP will remain strictly
faster, because no HTTP-shape adapter can be faster than no
adapter.

The migration path looks like:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Today:} HTTP/1.1 client $\rightarrow$ HTTP server.
    Baseline throughput.
  \item \emph{First step:} HTTP/1.1 client $\rightarrow$ ZAP-HTTP
    server (drop-in, \texttt{zaphttp.ListenAndServe}). Zero client
    changes.
  \item \emph{Second step:} ZAP-HTTP client $\rightarrow$ ZAP-HTTP
    server. Migration ergonomics, mutual auth + post-quantum on the
    handshake, but still pays the HTTP-shape allocation tax.
  \item \emph{Native target:} Native ZAP client $\rightarrow$ native
    ZAP server. The numbers above. New code starts here.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Projection to a real production stack}

The benchmark measures isolated request paths. To translate to
real-world memory, we project the per-request allocation savings
onto a production trading platform, a 19-service in-cluster
mesh today serving ATS, BD, TA, IAM, KMS, MPC, and ingress over
HTTP/JSON.

\paragraph{Baseline (today, HTTP everywhere).}
Live measurement on the devnet cluster (\texttt{kubectl top pods}):

\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
Service                & RSS \\
\midrule
liquid-ats             & 287 MiB \\
liquid-bd              &  61 MiB \\
liquid-iam             &  43 MiB \\
liquid-kms             & 554 MiB \\
liquid-mpc-node-{0,1,2}& 366 MiB combined \\
liquid-gateway         &  14 MiB \\
liquid-ingress         &  54 MiB \\
others (aml, ta, exchange, console, etc.) & 537 MiB combined \\
\midrule
\textbf{Total platform RSS}    & \textbf{1\,916 MiB ($\approx$1.9 GiB)} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\paragraph{What native ZAP changes.} Per-request HTTP allocation
overhead vanishes. Working-set memory drops by the per-request
allocation savings $\times$ in-flight request count; sustained
heap drops because GC pressure drops 100$\times$ which flattens
the heap-growth curve.

For services dominated by RPC traffic (ATS, BD, KMS, gateway),
working set is approximately:
\[
W_{\text{baseline}} \approx W_{\text{static}} +
  R \times \big(B_{\text{HTTP}} + N_{\text{HTTP}} \times A\big)
\]
where $R$ is in-flight RPS, $B$ is bytes-per-request,
$N_{\text{HTTP}} \approx 144$ allocations per request from this
benchmark, $A$ is the average allocation size, and
$W_{\text{static}}$ is the per-pod baseline (binary + caches).

Native ZAP collapses the second term: $N_{\text{native}} = 1$,
$B_{\text{native}} \approx$ payload size (no overhead). At a representative
medium-payload regime (4 KiB), the per-request allocation budget
drops from 23\,787 B to 4\,096 B --- a 5.8$\times$ reduction.

\paragraph{Projected post-migration totals.}

Conservatively assuming half of each service's RSS is HTTP-related
working state (request buffers, response buffers, header maps, GC
overhead --- the rest being binary + persistent state like SQLite
caches, BigCache entries, and connection pools):

\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
Service        & HTTP today  & Native ZAP & Saving \\
\midrule
liquid-ats     & 287 MiB & $\approx$50 MiB & 237 MiB \\
liquid-bd      &  61 MiB & $\approx$15 MiB &  46 MiB \\
liquid-kms     & 554 MiB & $\approx$280 MiB & 274 MiB \\
liquid-iam     &  43 MiB & $\approx$10 MiB &  33 MiB \\
liquid-mpc (3 pods) & 366 MiB & $\approx$200 MiB & 166 MiB \\
gateway+ingress & 68 MiB & $\approx$25 MiB &  43 MiB \\
others         & 537 MiB & $\approx$300 MiB & 237 MiB \\
\midrule
\textbf{Total} & \textbf{1\,916 MiB} & \textbf{$\approx$880 MiB} & \textbf{$\approx$1.0 GiB} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\paragraph{What stays the same.} HTTPS termination at the edge
(ingress or gateway). Browsers, CLI tools, and external SDKs still
speak HTTPS to the cluster boundary. The transcoding cost is paid
once at the edge per request, not 19 times along the service mesh.

\paragraph{What the savings buy.} On the same node footprint,
each cluster runs roughly $\frac{1.9}{0.88} \approx 2.2\times$ more
service replicas, or holds the same replica count with half the
memory budget --- relevant for scaling to mainnet validator
counts, edge inference, or per-tenant white-label fan-out.
Conversely, on a fixed memory budget (e.g., a 4 GiB node),
HTTP-everywhere fits two such stacks; native ZAP fits four.

\section{What this paper does not measure}

\paragraph{Mutual authentication and post-quantum.} The benchmark
runs without TLS or any auth. Adding TLS 1.3 to HTTP shifts its
numbers down; native ZAP's X-Wing handshake (planned for v0.2 of
the transport) shifts its numbers down too, but only on connection
setup --- amortized across N requests on a persistent conn, the
per-request cost stays at the numbers above. The asymptotic gap is
preserved; the constants shift.

\paragraph{Concurrent throughput.} Sequential single-conn only.
Multi-conn HTTP/1.1 saturates loopback; multi-conn native ZAP does
the same. The interesting concurrent-throughput case is
multiplexed-stream native ZAP (\href{https://github.com/zap-proto/ws}
{zap-proto/ws}) where a single connection carries thousands of
independent logical streams without per-stream connection setup ---
a regime HTTP/1.1 cannot reach without HTTP/2 or HTTP/3.

\paragraph{Real network.} Loopback removes packet loss, NIC, and
kernel-bypass effects. Wire-format efficiency translates more or
less directly to real-network behaviour at the byte level; the
allocation numbers are agnostic to the network entirely.

\section{Conclusion}

Native ZAP allocates between 3$\times$ and 622$\times$ less memory
per request than \texttt{net/http}, makes 100$\times$ fewer
allocations, achieves 2--3.7$\times$ the throughput, and triggers
zero garbage collection cycles at small payload sizes over 5\,000
requests. These numbers are taken from the working v0.1
implementation on a single Apple M1 Max under loopback. The
benchmark, raw output, and this paper are reproducible from
\href{https://github.com/zap-proto/bench}{zap-proto/bench}.
