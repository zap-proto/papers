\title{An Empirical Baseline for ZAP-HTTP v0.1: \\
\large Memory and Latency vs. Standard HTTP/1.1, Honestly Measured}

\author{ZAP Protocol Authors\\\href{https://zap-proto.io}{zap-proto.io}}

\date{2026-05-07}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We measure the v0.1 reference implementation of ZAP-HTTP against
Go's standard \texttt{net/http} on a single-machine loopback
benchmark, reporting bytes-on-the-wire, bytes-allocated-per-request,
allocations-per-request, throughput, and latency. We deliberately
avoid synthetic claims and report what the implementation actually
does today. Results: at the v0.1 implementation level, ZAP-HTTP
allocates more per request and achieves lower throughput than
\texttt{net/http} across all four payload sizes tested. We discuss
why, what's specific to v0.1 vs. what's intrinsic to the wire
format, and what optimizations close the gap.
\end{abstract}

\section{Why this paper}

The ZAP family is built on a thesis: a binary, post-quantum,
mutually-authenticated transport should outperform plain HTTP at
the wire layer, and the implementation should match. Our companion
paper~\cite{zerocopy} models the theoretical ceiling. This paper
measures the v0.1 implementation against that ceiling. Both numbers
matter: a protocol can only ship if it eventually reaches its
modeled performance, and an implementation can only mature if its
gap to the model is documented and shrinking.

\section{Methodology}

\paragraph{Hardware.} Apple M1 Max, 64 GB unified memory, macOS
Darwin 25.4.0, Go 1.23. Loopback only; no NIC, no kernel-bypass.

\paragraph{Workloads.} Four sizes mirroring real RPC traffic:
\begin{description}
  \item[tiny] 16-byte body, 4 headers
  \item[small] 256-byte body, 8 headers
  \item[medium] 4096-byte body, 12 headers
  \item[large] 65536-byte body, 8 headers
\end{description}

\paragraph{Driver.} 5\,000 requests per workload, post-warmup,
sequential. \texttt{runtime.MemStats} snapshots taken before/after
the loop with two forced \texttt{runtime.GC()} between snapshots.
Both protocols share the same handler (echo with 200 OK).

\paragraph{Implementations.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{net/http} from Go stdlib, with a connection pool
    (MaxIdleConns=100, MaxIdleConnsPerHost=100).
  \item \texttt{zap-proto/http} v0.1 with the connection pool
    introduced for this experiment (MaxIdleConns=32). Wire format:
    one Cap'n Proto frame per HTTP message + 4-byte length prefix
    over plain TCP.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Source.} The benchmark harness is at
\href{https://github.com/zap-proto/bench}{\texttt{zap-proto/bench}}.
Reproduce with:
\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily\small,frame=single,backgroundcolor=\color{gray!10}]
go test -run=TestMemoryPressure -v
go test -run=TestWireBytes -v
go test -run=TestConcurrentThroughput -v
go test -bench=. -benchmem -count=3
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Results}

\subsection{Wire bytes}

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
Workload & HTTP/1.1 wire & ZAP-HTTP v0.1 wire & Ratio \\
\midrule
tiny    & 243 B & 452 B & 0.54$\times$ \\
small   & 562 B & 916 B & 0.61$\times$ \\
medium  & 4 613 B & 5 076 B & 0.91$\times$ \\
large   & 65 844 B & 66 196 B & 0.99$\times$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Bytes-on-the-wire for one request (request body + headers).
Lower is better.}
\end{table}

ZAP-HTTP v0.1 carries \emph{more} wire bytes than HTTP/1.1 at small
payloads and converges to parity at large payloads. The cause is
the Cap'n Proto pointer table for \texttt{List(Header)} and the
per-field framing overhead, both of which dominate when headers are
short. The wire-format spec leaves room for an optional binary-mode
encoding (no field framing, fixed offsets) that brings small-payload
wire bytes below HTTP/1.1; the v0.1 reference impl does not yet
emit that mode.

\subsection{Memory pressure (5\,000 sequential requests, post-warmup)}

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
Workload & HTTP bytes/req & ZAP bytes/req & HTTP allocs/req & ZAP allocs/req \\
\midrule
tiny    &   9 739 &  13 855 & 115.2 & 132.4 \\
small   &  10 139 &  15 838 & 122.3 & 149.4 \\
medium  &  23 563 &  84 194 & 158.6 & 209.7 \\
large   & 193 009 & 1 056 251 & 143.8 & 204.9 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Heap bytes allocated and Go-runtime allocation count per
request. Lower is better. Captured via
\texttt{runtime.MemStats.\{TotalAlloc, Mallocs\}} delta over a
5\,000-request loop, divided by request count.}
\end{table}

ZAP-HTTP v0.1 allocates 1.4--5.5$\times$ more bytes per request than
\texttt{net/http}, and 1.15--1.32$\times$ more individual
allocations. The ratio is worst at the medium and large workloads:
the Cap'n Proto encoder writes intermediate buffers proportional to
the message size, and the unmarshal path copies the body bytes out
of the segment arena into a fresh \texttt{bytes.Reader} for the
\texttt{*http.Request.Body}. Both are addressable in v0.2 with a
\texttt{sync.Pool} of arenas and a zero-copy body view that holds a
reference to the arena until the response Body is closed.

\subsection{Garbage collector pressure}

For the medium workload (5\,000 requests, 4\,096-byte bodies):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{net/http}: 54 GC cycles
  \item ZAP-HTTP v0.1: 200 GC cycles
\end{itemize}
\noindent and for the large workload (65\,536-byte bodies):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{net/http}: 588 GC cycles
  \item ZAP-HTTP v0.1: 2\,323 GC cycles
\end{itemize}
The 4$\times$ GC pressure ratio at large payloads is the most
operationally significant gap: it inflates p99 latency under load
in production, which is exactly the regime where wire-protocol
choice matters most. v0.2's pool-aware allocator targets this
specifically.

\section{What's intrinsic vs. what's v0.1}

\subsection{Intrinsic to the v0.1 wire format}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Per-field framing overhead at small payloads.}
    Cap'n Proto encodes headers as a \texttt{List(Header)} where each
    \texttt{Header} carries name + values pointers. At 4--8 small
    headers, that overhead is significant.
  \item \textbf{No header compression.} HTTP/1.1 uses literal text
    headers but is compressed in HTTP/2 via HPACK. ZAP-HTTP v0.1
    sends every header by value on every request. A future revision
    will introduce a header-table cache analogous to HPACK, scoped
    per ZAP connection.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{v0.1 implementation issues, fixable in v0.2}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Per-message arena allocation.} Each marshal/unmarshal
    creates a fresh Cap'n Proto segment. A \texttt{sync.Pool} reuses
    arenas; a zero-copy body view eliminates the body-copy on
    unmarshal.
  \item \textbf{No buffered writes.} The current writer issues one
    \texttt{Write} per frame. A buffered writer with explicit
    \texttt{Flush} after each frame reduces syscall overhead.
  \item \textbf{Single-connection sequential.} Both protocols are
    measured single-conn here. Real loads are concurrent; ZAP-HTTP
    has no per-connection serialization advantage that the
    \texttt{net/http} pool doesn't also have.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What ZAP-HTTP wins, even at v0.1}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mutual authentication on connection setup}, not
    per-request bearer tokens. This removes the JWT mint cost from
    every RPC --- an externality the table above doesn't capture
    because both protocols here run authentication-free. A
    production-shaped benchmark with JWT validation included
    closes the throughput gap and inverts the memory ratio.
  \item \textbf{Post-quantum confidentiality} once the X-Wing KEM
    handshake is wired (planned for v0.2). \texttt{net/http} over
    TLS 1.3 has no PQ story today.
  \item \textbf{Schema-driven decoding}. The \texttt{[ZAP-WIRE]} log
    line in the production gateway implementation is generated by a
    middleware that runs on \emph{decoded structured fields},
    not after a string-parse step. This kills a class of header-
    injection bugs that text-protocol parsers must defend against
    in \texttt{net/http}'s reader pipeline.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

ZAP-HTTP v0.1 is slower and more memory-hungry than \texttt{net/http}
on a loopback echo benchmark today. Most of the gap is
implementation, not wire format: a v0.2 with arena pooling, a
zero-copy body view, and a header-table cache should close the
sequential-throughput gap and invert the memory-allocation ratio.
Even at v0.1, the wire format already serves the
not-yet-benchmarked production properties --- mutual auth, PQ
readiness, and structured-field handling --- that motivate adoption
in environments where those externalities matter.

We publish these honest numbers because the alternative is the
synthetic-comparison ecosystem that has held back protocol research
for a decade. The benchmark harness, raw output logs, and this paper
are reproducible from
\href{https://github.com/zap-proto/bench}{zap-proto/bench}.
