\title{Compositionality of the ZAP Protocol Family:\\
A Categorical Framework for Sub-Protocol Composition}
\author{ZAP Protocol Authors\\\texttt{zap-proto.io}}
\date{2026}

\begin{abstract}
The ZAP protocol family ships as a layered specification: a wire format
($\Zb$) under several application protocols (\texttt{zap-http},
\texttt{zap-ws}, \texttt{zap-fix}, \texttt{zap-rns}, \texttt{zap-mcp},
\texttt{zap-acp}, \texttt{zap-a2a}). Specifications of this shape are
usually composed informally, and the security guarantees of the parts
are not always preserved when they are stacked. We give a categorical
account of the family. We define a strict 2-category $\CZ$ whose
objects are protocols, whose 1-morphisms are session-preserving
embeddings, and whose 2-morphisms are simulation refinements. We prove
(i) $\CZ$ satisfies the category and 2-category axioms, (ii) $\Zb$ is
initial in $\CZ$, and (iii) every security property expressible as an
indistinguishability game with non-negligible advantage is preserved
along tight embeddings, with an explicit composition bound. The
framework makes mechanical the engineering claim that, e.g., running
\texttt{zap-fix-over-zap-rns} inherits the post-quantum and
session-binding properties of both layers without re-proof. We discuss
the relationship to Universal Composability~\cite{canetti2020uc},
multiparty session types~\cite{honda2016multiparty}, and reactive
simulatability~\cite{pfitzmann2001model}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Protocol stacks are usually composed at design time and verified
piecewise. The composition is left to a paragraph in the specification.
This is fine when the composition is loose --- e.g., HTTP-over-TCP,
where the application layer treats the transport as an opaque byte
stream. It is dangerous when the composition is tight --- where the
inner protocol relies on an invariant of the outer protocol that the
outer protocol does not promise. The triple-handshake attack on TLS
session resumption~\cite{bhargavan2014triple} is the canonical example:
the inner channel binding broke because two TLS sessions were chained
without a name binding.

The ZAP family is a layered specification with seven sub-protocols
sitting over a common wire format. The temptation in such a setting
is to write the spec in English and let implementers stitch the layers
together. We resist that temptation. We give a categorical framework
in which the composition is a primitive, security properties are
parameterized over morphisms, and the engineer's question
``\emph{if I run protocol $P$ over protocol $Q$, what do I get?}''
becomes a calculation rather than a discussion.

The contribution of this paper is structural, not cryptographic. We
do not prove new lower bounds for any individual protocol's security.
We give a category in which the existing proofs about the parts can
be \emph{composed} mechanically into proofs about the whole, with
explicit and tight bounds.

\section{The ZAP Sub-Protocol Family}
\label{sec:family}

We give a one-paragraph recap of each sub-protocol. Each is the
subject of a separate paper in this collection; we cite those for
the full specification and security claims.

\paragraph{$\Zb$ (zap wire).} The base wire format. A length-prefixed,
zero-copy frame carrying a typed envelope and a payload. Sessions are
identified by 128-bit session IDs and are bound to the underlying
transport via a key schedule derived from a hybrid post-quantum
KEM~\cite{zap2026pq}. $\Zb$ is the substrate; every other protocol in
the family ultimately produces $\Zb$ frames on the wire.

\paragraph{$\Zh$ (zap-http).} A request/response protocol. Each
request is a single $\Zb$ frame; each response is a single $\Zb$
frame. Idempotency is carried in the envelope. $\Zh$ has no
session-level state beyond the underlying $\Zb$ session.

\paragraph{$\Zw$ (zap-ws).} A bidirectional streaming protocol over a
single $\Zb$ session. Frames flow in both directions concurrently.
$\Zw$ adds back-pressure annotations and stream multiplexing identifiers.

\paragraph{$\Zf$ (zap-fix).} A FIX-compatible session protocol. Sequence
numbers, gap-fill, persistent session identifiers. $\Zf$ carries FIX
4.4-style messages re-encoded as $\Zb$ envelopes.

\paragraph{$\Zr$ (zap-rns).} The identity-binding sub-protocol. Names
are bound to public keys and to session keys at handshake time. $\Zr$
contributes the channel-binding token and is the source of the
``identity'' field in the $\Zb$ envelope of every authenticated
session~\cite{zap2026rns}.

\paragraph{$\Zm$ (zap-mcp).} A model context protocol carrying tool
calls and structured tool responses. Streaming responses are natural;
$\Zm$ runs as a $\Zw$ stream with structured envelopes.

\paragraph{$\Zac$ (zap-acp).} An agent control protocol: capability
negotiation, session establishment between agents, and structured
control messages.

\paragraph{$\Za$ (zap-a2a).} Agent-to-agent task delegation. A task is
a structured envelope; we will see that $\Za$ embeds in $\Zm$ by
viewing a task as an MCP tool call with structured arguments.

\section{The Category $\CZ$}
\label{sec:category}

\subsection{Protocols as Objects}

We work in a labeled transition system model. A protocol $P$ is a
tuple $(\Sigma_P, \mathcal{S}_P, s^0_P, \to_P, \mathcal{V}_P)$ where
$\Sigma_P$ is the message alphabet (set of well-formed envelopes),
$\mathcal{S}_P$ is the set of session states, $s^0_P$ is the initial
state, $\to_P \subseteq \mathcal{S}_P \times \Sigma_P \times
\mathcal{S}_P$ is the labelled transition relation, and
$\mathcal{V}_P : \mathcal{S}_P \to 2^{\mathrm{Prop}}$ is a valuation
over a fixed propositional vocabulary that includes the security
predicates we care about (confidentiality, authenticity,
session-bound, post-quantum, fresh-key, channel-bound, etc.).

A \emph{trace} of $P$ is a finite sequence $\sigma = m_1 m_2 \cdots
m_n \in \Sigma_P^*$ such that there exist states $s_0, s_1, \ldots,
s_n$ with $s_0 = s^0_P$ and $s_{i-1} \xrightarrow{m_i}_P s_i$ for all
$i$. Write $\mathrm{Tr}(P)$ for the set of all traces and
$\mathrm{Tr}^\omega(P)$ for the set of infinite traces.

\subsection{Embeddings as 1-Morphisms}

\begin{definition}[Protocol embedding]
\label{def:embedding}
A \emph{protocol embedding} from $P$ to $Q$, written
$\varphi : P \hookrightarrow Q$, is a triple $(\varphi_\Sigma,
\varphi_\mathcal{S}, \rho)$ such that:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\varphi_\Sigma : \Sigma_P \to \Sigma_Q^+$ is an injective map
that translates each $P$-message into a non-empty finite sequence of
$Q$-messages.
\item $\varphi_\mathcal{S} : \mathcal{S}_P \to \mathcal{S}_Q$ is a
state map with $\varphi_\mathcal{S}(s^0_P) = s^0_Q$ when $\rho =
\bot$, or $\varphi_\mathcal{S}(s^0_P)$ is reachable from $s^0_Q$
when $\rho \neq \bot$.
\item Whenever $s \xrightarrow{m}_P s'$, the unique trace
$\varphi_\Sigma(m) = m_1 m_2 \cdots m_k$ produces transitions
$\varphi_\mathcal{S}(s) \xrightarrow{m_1}_Q \cdots
\xrightarrow{m_k}_Q \varphi_\mathcal{S}(s')$.
\item For every security predicate $\pi$ in the shared vocabulary,
$\pi \in \mathcal{V}_P(s) \implies \pi \in
\mathcal{V}_Q(\varphi_\mathcal{S}(s))$ \emph{whenever the embedding
declares $\pi$ as preserved}; we write $\Pi(\varphi)$ for the set of
such $\pi$.
\item $\rho$ is the \emph{embedding's leakage profile}: an upper bound
on the additional information that an observer of $Q$ learns about
the $P$-session relative to an observer of $P$ alone. Formally,
$\rho \in [0,1]$ bounds the statistical distance between the
distribution of $Q$-traces under $\varphi$ and the distribution that
would result from running $Q$ on uniformly random input of the same
length.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

The leakage profile $\rho$ is the categorical placeholder for the
``cost of embedding'': for tight embeddings, $\rho = 0$.

\begin{definition}[Composition]
\label{def:composition}
Given $\varphi : P \hookrightarrow Q$ and $\psi : Q \hookrightarrow
R$, the composition $\psi \circ \varphi : P \hookrightarrow R$ is
$(\psi_\Sigma^* \circ \varphi_\Sigma,\, \psi_\mathcal{S} \circ
\varphi_\mathcal{S},\, \rho_\varphi + \rho_\psi)$, where
$\psi_\Sigma^*$ extends $\psi_\Sigma$ to sequences componentwise.
\end{definition}

The leakage adds in the standard sub-additive sense (statistical
distance is a metric and obeys the triangle inequality); we use
addition as a tight upper bound for the small leakages encountered
in the family.

\begin{definition}[Identity]
The identity embedding $\mathrm{id}_P : P \hookrightarrow P$ is
$(\mathrm{id}_{\Sigma_P}^{(1)}, \mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{S}_P}, 0)$,
where $\mathrm{id}_\Sigma^{(1)}(m) = m$ as a one-element sequence.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Refinements as 2-Morphisms}

\begin{definition}[Simulation refinement]
\label{def:refinement}
Given two embeddings $\varphi, \varphi' : P \hookrightarrow Q$, a
\emph{simulation refinement} $\alpha : \varphi \Rightarrow \varphi'$
is a binary relation
$R_\alpha \subseteq \mathcal{S}_Q \times \mathcal{S}_Q$ such that:
\begin{enumerate}
\item For all $s \in \mathcal{S}_P$,
$(\varphi_\mathcal{S}(s), \varphi'_\mathcal{S}(s)) \in R_\alpha$.
\item Whenever $(t, t') \in R_\alpha$ and $t \xrightarrow{m}_Q t_1$
in the image of $\varphi$, there exists a $Q$-trace
$t' \xrightarrow{m'_1}_Q \cdots \xrightarrow{m'_j}_Q t'_j$ in the
image of $\varphi'$ such that $(t_1, t'_j) \in R_\alpha$ and the
two paths are observationally equivalent up to the difference in
leakage $\rho_{\varphi'} - \rho_\varphi$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

A 2-morphism captures the engineering reality that two embeddings
of the same protocol may differ in their wire-level realization
without differing in the abstract semantics they realize.

\subsection{Category Axioms}

\begin{theorem}[Category and 2-category]
\label{thm:cat}
The data $(\mathrm{Obj}(\CZ), \mathrm{Hom}_{\CZ}, \mathrm{2Hom}_{\CZ},
\circ, \mathrm{id})$ defines a strict 2-category. In particular,
1-composition is associative and unital, and 2-cells compose
horizontally and vertically with the standard interchange law.
\end{theorem}

The proof is in the companion document
(Theorem~1, \texttt{proofs/composability/proof.tex}). The unfolding is straightforward; the only
non-trivial check is that the leakage profiles compose by addition
across all three axes (vertical, horizontal, identity), which they
do because statistical distance is a metric.

\section{Composition Theorem}
\label{sec:composition}

\subsection{Tight Embeddings}

\begin{definition}[Tight embedding]
\label{def:tight}
An embedding $\varphi : P \hookrightarrow Q$ is \emph{tight} if its
leakage profile satisfies $\rho_\varphi \le \mathrm{negl}(\lambda)$,
where $\lambda$ is the security parameter and $\mathrm{negl}$ denotes
any function negligible in $\lambda$.
\end{definition}

The intuition is that an observer of the outer protocol $Q$ learns
nothing more about the inner protocol $P$ than they would learn from
a $P$-observer with access to a uniformly random tape of the same
length. The condition rules out side-channel leakage by virtue of the
embedding itself; it does not rule out side-channels that come from
implementations.

\subsection{Inheritance}

We model security properties as indistinguishability games. A property
$\pi$ over protocol $P$ is captured by a game $G_\pi^P$ between a
challenger and an adversary $\mathcal{A}$, with advantage
$\Adv_\pi^P(\mathcal{A}) := |\Pr[G_\pi^P(\mathcal{A}) = 1] - 1/2|$.
The property holds against a class $\mathcal{C}$ of adversaries with
parameter $\lambda$ if $\Adv_\pi^P(\mathcal{A}) \le
\mathrm{negl}(\lambda)$ for all $\mathcal{A} \in \mathcal{C}$.

\begin{theorem}[Composition of security under tight embedding]
\label{thm:composition}
Let $\varphi : P \hookrightarrow Q$ be a tight embedding and let
$\pi \in \Pi(\varphi)$. If $P$ satisfies $\pi$ against adversary
class $\mathcal{C}$ with bound $\epsilon_P(\lambda)$, then $Q$
satisfies $\pi$ against the same class with bound
$\epsilon_Q(\lambda) \le \epsilon_P(\lambda) + \rho_\varphi
+ \delta_\varphi(\lambda)$, where $\delta_\varphi$ is a function of
the embedding's complexity (see Theorem~3 of the proof companion).
For tight embeddings, $\rho_\varphi$ is negligible and $Q$ inherits
$\pi$ up to a negligible additive term.
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[Stack inheritance]
\label{cor:stack}
If $\varphi_1 : P_1 \hookrightarrow P_2$, $\varphi_2 : P_2
\hookrightarrow P_3$, \ldots, $\varphi_n : P_n \hookrightarrow
P_{n+1}$ are all tight, and $P_1$ satisfies $\pi$ with bound
$\epsilon_1$, then $P_{n+1}$ satisfies $\pi$ with bound bounded by
$\epsilon_1 + \sum_{i=1}^n (\rho_{\varphi_i} + \delta_{\varphi_i})$,
which is negligible whenever the chain length $n$ is polynomial in
$\lambda$.
\end{corollary}

The proof is by induction on the chain length, with the inductive
step being a direct application of \cref{thm:composition}.

\section{Concrete Embeddings}
\label{sec:instances}

We exhibit five embeddings used by the family. Each is shown to be
tight in the companion proofs document.

\subsection{$\Zh \hookrightarrow \Zw$: HTTP as single-stream WS}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:http-ws}
There is a tight embedding $\varphi_{hw} : \Zh \hookrightarrow \Zw$
that maps a request/response pair to a singleton bidirectional stream
opened, used for one round trip, and closed.
\end{proposition}

The embedding takes a $\Zh$ request frame to a $\Zw$ \texttt{OPEN}
frame followed by the same envelope, and a $\Zh$ response frame to a
$\Zw$ envelope followed by a \texttt{CLOSE} frame. The leakage is
zero because the only additional bytes are framing bytes that an
$\Zh$-only observer would already see at the $\Zb$ layer.

\subsection{$\Zf \hookrightarrow \Zw$: FIX session as persistent stream}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:fix-ws}
There is a tight embedding $\varphi_{fw} : \Zf \hookrightarrow \Zw$
that maps a FIX session to a single $\Zw$ stream, with FIX sequence
numbers preserved as $\Zw$ envelope sequence numbers.
\end{proposition}

The map is straightforward: each FIX message is a $\Zw$ envelope, and
the FIX session-level reset semantics are realized by $\Zw$
\texttt{RESET} frames. The post-quantum properties of the underlying
$\Zb$ key schedule are inherited; $\Zf$ does not contribute its own
key schedule.

\subsection{$\Zr \hookrightarrow \Zb$: RNS into the base envelope}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:rns-base}
There is a tight embedding $\varphi_{rb} : \Zr \hookrightarrow \Zb$
that injects an RNS-bound identity into the \texttt{identity} field
of the $\Zb$ envelope at handshake time.
\end{proposition}

This is the most direct embedding in the family; $\Zr$ contributes
exactly one piece of state (the bound identity) and one piece of wire
(the channel-binding token), both of which live natively in $\Zb$.

\subsection{$\Zm \hookrightarrow \Zw$: MCP over WS}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:mcp-ws}
There is a tight embedding $\varphi_{mw} : \Zm \hookrightarrow \Zw$
that maps an MCP tool-call/streaming-response pair to a $\Zw$ stream
where the request is a single envelope and the response is a finite
sequence of envelopes terminated by a final-frame marker.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{$\Za \hookrightarrow \Zm$: A2A as MCP tool calls}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:a2a-mcp}
There is a tight embedding $\varphi_{am} : \Za \hookrightarrow \Zm$
that maps an A2A task to an MCP tool call with structured arguments
and an MCP streaming response carrying the task progress and result.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}
The embedding $\Za \hookrightarrow \Zm \hookrightarrow \Zw
\hookrightarrow \Zb$ makes precise the claim
``\texttt{zap-a2a} runs over \texttt{zap-mcp} runs over
\texttt{zap-ws} runs over the wire.'' The composite embedding is
tight by~\cref{cor:stack}, and so the post-quantum, channel-binding,
and authenticity properties of $\Zb$ are inherited by $\Za$ without a
separate proof.
\end{remark}

\section{Universal Property of $\Zb$}
\label{sec:universal}

\begin{theorem}[Initial object]
\label{thm:initial}
$\Zb$ is initial in $\CZ$: for every protocol $P \in
\mathrm{Obj}(\CZ)$, there exists a unique morphism
$\iota_P : \Zb \hookrightarrow P$ given by ``every $\Zb$ envelope is a
well-formed $P$-message in the boot phase before any $P$-specific
state is added.''
\end{theorem}

The proof is in the companion document (Theorem~2 of the proof
companion). Initiality formalizes a phrase from the family's design notes:
``\emph{The wire is the source of truth.}'' Concretely:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Any parser that handles $\Zb$ frames can recover the structured
semantics of any sub-protocol $P$ by composing with the unique
$\iota_P$.
\item Any new sub-protocol added to the family must define an
embedding into $\Zb$. The category enforces it.
\item Wire-level interoperability across language bindings is a
functor: each binding $L$ is a category $\mathcal{C}_L$ of
``protocol-as-implemented-in-$L$,'' and there is a functor $F_L :
\CZ \to \mathcal{C}_L$ that takes $\Zb$ to its in-language envelope
type. By initiality, every sub-protocol's binding type is determined
up to isomorphism by the binding for $\Zb$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Practical Implications}
\label{sec:practice}

\subsection{Security review reduces to embedding review}

For a new sub-protocol $P_{\mathrm{new}}$, the security review need
not re-prove handshake security, post-quantum properties, or
channel-binding. It suffices to:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Define the embedding $\iota_{P_{\mathrm{new}}} : \Zb
\hookrightarrow P_{\mathrm{new}}$ (always exists by initiality).
\item Show the embedding is tight.
\item Identify the security properties $\pi$ that $\Zb$ already
provides and that the embedding preserves
($\pi \in \Pi(\iota_{P_{\mathrm{new}}})$).
\item Prove only the $P_{\mathrm{new}}$-specific properties on top.
\end{enumerate}

This is a substantial reduction in proof burden. For example, when
$\Zm$ was added to the family, the security review consisted of
showing that the MCP-over-WS embedding is tight (a one-page proof,
\cref{prop:mcp-ws}) and that MCP's tool-call authorization adds a
single new property on top of the inherited $\Zb$ guarantees. No
re-proof of the post-quantum hybrid KEM was needed.

\subsection{Wire compatibility}

A $\Zb$ parser is universal in the family. Tooling (Wireshark
dissectors~\cite{zap2026wire}, language bindings, capture analyzers)
need to be written once at the $\Zb$ layer; the structured semantics
of any sub-protocol are recovered by composition with the relevant
embedding.

\subsection{Implementation languages as functors}

The 30+ language bindings under \texttt{zap-go}, \texttt{zap-rust},
\texttt{zap-java}, and so on are each a functor $F_L : \CZ \to
\mathcal{C}_L$. The categorical structure is preserved: an embedding
$\varphi : P \hookrightarrow Q$ in $\CZ$ becomes an embedding
$F_L(\varphi)$ in $\mathcal{C}_L$. This means cross-language
interoperability is automatic at the categorical level: an A2A
agent written in Rust calling an MCP server written in Go is a
diagram in $\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{wire}}$ that commutes by
functoriality.

\subsection{Stack security in one example}

\begin{example}[Inherited PQ properties for FIX-over-RNS]
\label{ex:fix-pq}
Consider an institutional client running $\Zf$ (FIX-compatible
session) over $\Zr$ (identity binding) over $\Zw$ over $\Zb$. The
chain of embeddings is
$\Zf \hookrightarrow \Zw \hookrightarrow \Zb \leftarrow \Zr
\hookrightarrow \Zb$, with the $\Zr$ contribution an additive
binding rather than a layering. By~\cref{cor:stack} and the
$\Zr \hookrightarrow \Zb$ tightness, the post-quantum hybrid-KEM key
schedule of $\Zb$ is inherited by $\Zf$, and the identity-binding
guarantee of $\Zr$ holds at every $\Zf$ message. No FIX-specific
post-quantum proof is needed.
\end{example}

\subsection{Where the framework does not help}

The framework gives no help against side-channel attacks below the
LTS abstraction (timing, cache, electromagnetic), against
implementation bugs that violate the tightness condition (e.g., an
implementation that leaks session state via debug logs), or against
adversaries outside the modeled class (e.g., adversaries with
access to the host's memory). It also says nothing about
\emph{availability} composition; a denial-of-service attack on $\Zb$
denies service to every sub-protocol.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Universal Composability.}
Canetti's UC framework~\cite{canetti2001uc, canetti2020uc} is the
gold standard for composable cryptographic security. Our framework is
strictly weaker: we model protocols as labelled transition systems
and security properties as indistinguishability games, without the
UC machinery of environments, ideal functionalities, and the
composition theorem. The trade is deliberate: UC proofs are
expensive, and the ZAP family does not need the full strength of UC
because the embeddings are tight by construction. For sub-protocols
where UC-strength composition is required (e.g., $\Zr$ when used as
a stand-alone identity provider), a UC proof is given separately
and the categorical framework is used to lift it to the rest of the
family.

\paragraph{Multiparty session types.}
Honda, Yoshida, and Carbone~\cite{honda2016multiparty} give a type
system in which multiparty protocols are specified globally and
projected to local types per participant. Our category $\CZ$ can be
viewed as a coarser, ``protocol-level'' analogue of session types:
objects are protocols rather than session types per se, and morphisms
are embeddings rather than projections. We do not pursue the
projection direction here; that would be the natural follow-up.

\paragraph{Reactive simulatability.}
Pfitzmann and Waidner~\cite{pfitzmann2000reactive, pfitzmann2001model}
develop a model close in spirit to UC but with an explicit notion of
reactive systems and a composition theorem in that setting. Our
2-cells (simulation refinements) are inspired by their notion of
``perfect indistinguishability of structures.''

\paragraph{Process algebra.}
CSP~\cite{hoare1985csp} and the
$\pi$-calculus~\cite{milner1999pi} provide the LTS substrate we work
in. Our embeddings are a categorical variant of CSP-style refinement
under hiding, with the security predicates added on top.

\paragraph{Categorical accounts of protocols.}
Caires and Pfenning's session types as
intuitionistic linear propositions~\cite{caires2010session} and
Wadler's ``Propositions as Sessions''~\cite{wadler2012propositions}
give a categorical foundation for session-typed concurrency. We work
at a coarser level (protocol families rather than individual
sessions); the two views are complementary.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The ZAP family forms a strict 2-category $\CZ$. The base wire format
$\Zb$ is initial. Tight embeddings preserve security properties under
composition with explicit and tight bounds. The seven sub-protocols
embed into one another along the diagram of~\cref{sec:instances}, and
the practical engineering claim that any composite stack inherits
the security of its components is, in the framework, a calculation.
Future work includes (i) a Coq formalization of $\CZ$ with the
embeddings of \cref{sec:instances} mechanically verified, (ii) the
projection from $\CZ$ to a multiparty session-type
calculus~\cite{honda2016multiparty}, and (iii) a denotational
semantics in which the leakage profile $\rho$ is given by an
information-theoretic mutual-information bound rather than by
statistical distance.
